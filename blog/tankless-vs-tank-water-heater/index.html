    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in North Dakota

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

    When the old heater lets go, the first question is always the same. Do you put in another tank, or is this the time to go tankless? National buying guides skip the local conditions, and those conditions decide more than the brochure does. Incoming water temperature, how hot or cold the garage runs and how much mineral is in the water change the answer, sometimes by a lot, so it pays to know them before you sign anything.

    Big swings between summer heat and winter cold make pipes expand and contract, which slowly loosens fittings over the years.

    How Each Type Works

    A tank heater stores 40 to 75 gallons and keeps it hot around the clock with a gas burner or electric elements. Plumbers have installed them for a century, so parts and labor are cheap. The tradeoff is standby loss, the energy spent reheating water nobody is using. Drain it and you wait.

    A tankless unit heats water only when a fixture calls for it. Water passes through a heat exchanger, a flow sensor fires the burner, and hot water keeps coming as long as the tap is open. Capacity is rated in gallons per minute at a given temperature rise. Tank size does not apply. Whole-house tankless is mostly gas, since electric models that size can call for 100 amps or more on their own.

    How Local Conditions Change the Math

    Tankless output depends on how many degrees the heater has to add. Where the cold water coming in is already fairly warm, a unit can push a higher flow rate. In warm climates the incoming water stays mild even in winter. A smaller rise means more gallons per minute. Where the supply water is cold most of the year, sizing gets a lot less forgiving.

    Garage temperature counts too. A tank in a hot garage loses less heat to standby, which narrows the tankless efficiency edge in summer. Those same hot garages make hybrid heat pump water heaters work very well, since they pull heat out of the surrounding air and knock a few degrees off the garage while they run. In a cold basement or garage a heat pump unit struggles.

    Hard water hits tankless units hardest. Scale builds fast on the heat exchanger, and most manufacturers require yearly descaling to keep the warranty. Tank heaters suffer as well, with sediment cutting years off their life and making that popping noise you hear when the burner fires. A softener or scale inhibitor ahead of the heater pays off either way.

    Water in North Dakota is often very hard, with high mineral and sulfate content that builds scale in water heaters and fixtures.

    Comparing the Costs

    Prices move with fuel type, venting and code upgrades. Here are typical installed ranges:

    • Standard gas or electric tank, 40 to 50 gallons: about $1,400 to $2,800 installed.
    • Whole-house gas tankless: about $3,000 to $6,000 installed, higher when the gas line needs upsizing.
    • Hybrid heat pump tank: about $2,800 to $5,000 installed, and rebates or federal tax credits can take a bite out of that.
    • Yearly tankless descaling: about $150 to $300 if a plumber does it.
    • Tank flush: about $100 to $200, once a year in hard water.

    The gas line is the cost people miss. A whole-house tankless can draw 180,000 BTU or more, and lines sized for a 40,000 BTU tank are often too small. Switching from tank to tankless usually also means new stainless or PVC venting, a condensate drain on condensing models, and a 120 volt outlet for the control board. A good quote lists each of those as its own line item.

    Which One Should You Choose?

    Budget replacements and rentals still call for a tank. It also fits houses where the gas line and venting are not set up for tankless. Tankless is a good fit for bigger families who run out of hot water and for owners planning to stay, and for anyone who wants that corner of the garage back. In an all-electric home with the heater in a warm garage, a hybrid heat pump is worth a serious look. Expect a good tank to last 8 to 12 years on hard water and a maintained tankless 15 to 20.

    When to Call a Professional

    Water heater installs touch gas piping, venting and combustion air, along with the expansion tank and the T and P relief line. Every one of those gets checked at inspection.
