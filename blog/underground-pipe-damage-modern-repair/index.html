    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Goes Wrong Under the Yard

    Your sewer lateral and water service run from the house to the street, usually somewhere 2 to 6 feet underground. Nobody thinks about them until a toilet backs up or the lawn turns soggy. Most laterals are 4-inch, and on older houses that usually means clay or cast iron. Each fails in its own way.

    Common Causes

    Tree Roots

    Roots find sewer lines by following moisture from a cracked joint or a loose gasket. Once a hair-thin root gets in, it grows into a mat that catches paper and grease. Clay pipe is the worst for this. The joints every two or three feet give roots a way in, and a mature oak or maple can fill a 4-inch line in a few seasons.

    Shifting Soil

    Frost heave can shift soil around sewer lines and crack joints, which often shows up as slow drains or backups.

    Soil that settles or swells moves the pipe with it. A rigid clay or cast iron line cannot flex, so it cracks or pulls apart at a joint. Sags are common too. A belly in the line holds standing water and waste, and the camera shows it as a section that stays full after a flush.

    Old Pipe Material

    Older homes may still have galvanized supply lines or cast iron drains, which can rust and restrict flow with age.

    Age wears every material down. Cast iron rusts from the inside, and the bottom of the pipe can corrode through while the top still looks solid. Orangeburg pipe, used through the early 1970s, is pressed tar paper that slowly flattens into an oval. Old galvanized water lines rust shut inside and drop the pressure everywhere.

    Signs of a Buried Line Problem

    Several drains slowing at once points to the main line. A gurgling toilet when the washing machine drains is a classic sign. In the yard, watch for a strip of grass that is greener than the rest, or ground that feels soft over the line during dry weather. Sewer smell near the cleanout is another.

    On the water side, the meter tells the story. Turn off every fixture and watch the leak indicator, the small triangle or dial on the meter face. When that indicator spins, water is escaping between the meter and the house, often from the buried service. Low pressure throughout the house can mean the same.

    A sewer camera settles it. We run a camera through the cleanout and record the whole line, with a locator that marks depth and position on the ground above. You see the same video we do. The footage decides the repair.

    Modern Repair Options

    Trench repair is still the right call for some jobs. A collapsed section or a line with a deep belly usually has to be dug up, since lining needs a pipe that still holds its shape. Spot repairs can be small. A single broken joint under the yard might mean a 4 by 6 foot hole and a day of work.

    Trenchless lining avoids most of the digging. A felt liner soaked in epoxy resin is pulled into the line, inflated, and cured into a pipe within the pipe. Usually it goes in through the cleanout plus one small pit, and it is done in a day. Roots cannot get through it.

    Pipe bursting is the other trenchless method. A bursting head pulled through the old line breaks it apart and drags new HDPE pipe into place right behind it. You need a pit on each end, but the lawn in between stays untouched. Our crews at HPN Plumbing use both methods across North Dakota, depending on what the camera shows.

    When to Call

    Do not wait on a sewer line that has backed up twice. Each backup leaves waste in the line, and roots grow faster with that steady food supply. Most of our camera inspections take under an hour, and you get a copy of the video plus a marked-up location of the problem. Reach HPN Plumbing at (701) 403-1348. It helps if you know where your cleanout is, because that saves time on the visit.
